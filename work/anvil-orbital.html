01Problem

Very large satellite fleets are being planned. When a low-orbit satellite retires today, it usually burns up or stays up as a dead object. We wanted to find out whether some of them are worth more in orbit.

Public catalogs list about 500 to 1,900 dead satellites in low orbit, depending on the catalog (CelesTrak 529, GCAT 1,878). Nobody has checked each one with its owner.

02What we built

The concept: with their owners’ agreement, tugs would bring retired satellites to a station in low orbit, where robots service them. A high-power satellite gets a compute module and goes back out as a data center node; the rest are recycled for parts. None of that hardware exists, so we started with software anyone can use today.

  • Fleet Retirement Planner: enter a fleet’s numbers and four answers about its retirement update, with a decay chart.
  • Simulator: real fleets (Starlink, OneWeb, Amazon Leo, Planet) drawn from a CelesTrak data snapshot.
  • Findings: our desk research and models, including the parts that argue against the idea.
  • Dead Satellite Reach, the first demo: which dead satellites one station could reach.

The site is static, with no tracking or forms.

03Who does what

Anvil Orbital is a two-founder project, started on 2 November 2026 by two high school students in Ontario. Hudson, my co-founder, leads the non-technical side. I build the software.

We use AI coding and research tools, and we say on the site where nobody has checked the results.

04What we found

Collecting dead satellites for their hardware doesn’t pay: about $18.6M per satellite in our model, against $46k–$116k for the reusable parts.

  • Even with every input in its favour, the model still costs about $3M per satellite.
  • The parts estimate assumes a third of a 700 kg satellite is reusable, at launch prices of $200–500/kg.
  • We found no completed example of a robot capturing a satellite in low orbit that wasn’t built for it.
  • So we stopped asking about salvage and started asking about retirement: do fleet designers see it as an open problem someone would pay to solve?

05Where it stands

  • Idea stage. No company has been formed and we’ve taken no investment.
  • Nobody has agreed to pay.
  • No spacecraft engineer has checked the models, and we don’t have one on the team yet.
  • We’re under 18, so an adult would need to sign any contract.
